African state systems likewise showed continuity, innovation, and diversity. In the western Sudan, control of gold, taxation of trans-Saharan trade, and Islam as a court religion supported large states.

  • Mali Empire — Mansa Musa’s famous hajj (1324) displayed gold wealth and Islamic piety; Timbuktu became a center of trade and scholarship.
  • Earlier Ghana’s pattern of taxing gold-salt trade continued in new forms under Mali and later Songhai.
  • Hausa city-states combined commerce, crafts, and Islamizing elites.

On the Swahili Coast, independent city-states (Kilwa, Mombasa, Mogadishu) spoke a Bantu language infused with Arabic (Swahili) and linked African gold, ivory, and enslaved people to Indian Ocean markets in exchange for cloth, porcelain, and spices.

Great Zimbabwe’s stone capital controlled interior gold and cattle wealth feeding that coastal trade. Ethiopian (Solomonic) Christian kings maintained a literate Christian state in the highlands — a continuity of African Christianity dating to Aksum.

Islam spread with merchants and scholars across the Sahara and along the East African coast, often remaining stronger among elites than among all rural people. Christianity continued in Ethiopia. Indigenous spiritual systems, ancestor veneration, and kingship rituals remained central in many societies.

African states used kinship, military aristocracies, control of trade nodes, and religious prestige rather than a single bureaucratic model copied from China or Europe.
