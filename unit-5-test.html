Excerpt from Jean-Jacques Rousseau, The Social Contract (1762):

"Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains. Those who think themselves the masters of others are indeed greater slaves than they. How did this change come about? I do not know. What can make it legitimate? That question I think I can answer. The social order is a sacred right which is the basis of all rights. Nevertheless, this right does not come from nature, and must therefore be founded on conventions."

Source: Jean-Jacques Rousseau, The Social Contract, translated by G.D.H. Cole, 1913 (as cited in[https://www.gutenberg.org/files/46333/46333-h/46333-h.htm](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/46333/46333-h/46333-h.htm)).

1. Based on the passage, Rousseau’s central argument about political authority most directly challenged which Enlightenment-era idea?

Explanation: Rousseau argues that legitimate authority comes from conventions (social contract), not nature or divine inheritance, directly undermining absolute monarchy.

2. The phrase “Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains” best reflects which broader Enlightenment theme?

Explanation: Rousseau emphasizes that freedom is natural but corrupted by artificial social and political structures.

3. Rousseau’s ideas in the passage most directly influenced which later historical development?

Explanation: Rousseau’s social contract theory provided intellectual justification for revolutionary demands that sovereignty resides in the people.

4. Which of the following best illustrates the broader impact of Enlightenment political philosophy, as expressed in the passage, during the period c. 1750–1900?

Explanation: Rousseau’s ideas helped fuel revolutions that questioned absolute rule and promoted popular sovereignty.

Excerpt from Toussaint Louverture’s Letter to the French Directory (1797):

"Brothers and friends, I am Toussaint Louverture... I have undertaken to restore to my fellow citizens their rights... You have given us liberty; we will defend it... We are not the enemies of France; on the contrary, we love her... But we will not suffer any attempt to reduce us again to slavery."

Source: Toussaint Louverture, Letter to the French Directory, 1797 (as cited in[https://www.britannica.com/biography/Toussaint-Louverture](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Toussaint-Louverture)).

1. Based on the passage, Toussaint Louverture’s primary goal in the Haitian Revolution was to

Explanation: Louverture explicitly states he is restoring rights and will not allow a return to slavery while still expressing attachment to French revolutionary ideals.

2. The passage’s reference to both “liberty” and loyalty to France illustrates which characteristic of many Atlantic revolutions?

Explanation: Louverture embraces liberty but frames it within continued (though reformed) ties to France.

3. Toussaint Louverture’s leadership most directly contributed to which outcome of the Haitian Revolution?

Explanation: Under Louverture’s leadership, Haiti achieved independence in 1804 after defeating French, Spanish, and British forces.

4. Which of the following best illustrates the broader impact of nationalist and revolutionary movements like the Haitian Revolution during the period c. 1750–1900?

Explanation: Haiti’s success influenced later Latin American independence movements and abolitionist thought.

Excerpt from Friedrich Engels, The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845):

"The great towns are chiefly inhabited by the working classes... The streets are generally unpaved... The houses are built back to back... The atmosphere is thick with smoke... The working people... are packed into these dens... In Manchester... there are often ten or twelve persons in a single room..."

Source: Friedrich Engels, The Condition of the Working Class in England, 1845 (as cited in[https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1845/condition-working-class/](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1845/condition-working-class/)).

1. Based on the passage, early industrialization in Britain most directly led to

Explanation: Engels describes densely packed, unsanitary urban housing resulting from factory-driven migration to cities.

2. The description of smoke-filled air and back-to-back housing highlights which environmental consequence of early industrialization?

Explanation: The passage emphasizes smoke and cramped housing as direct results of industrial growth.

3. Engels’ focus on working-class living conditions most strongly reflects which emerging intellectual response to industrialization?

Explanation: Engels’ detailed critique of working-class misery helped shape socialist thought and calls for reform.

4. Which of the following best illustrates the broader impact of early industrialization in Britain, as described in the passage, during the period c. 1750–1900?

Explanation: Conditions described by Engels fueled socialism, Chartism, and later labor reforms.

Excerpt from the Meiji Emperor’s Charter Oath (1868):

"By this oath, we set up as our aim the establishment of the national weal on a broad basis and the framing of a constitution and laws.

Deliberative assemblies shall be established and all measures shall be decided in accordance with public opinion.

All classes of the people shall unite in vigorously carrying on the affairs of the government.

All common people... shall be allowed to pursue their own calling... Knowledge shall be sought throughout the world..."

Source: Meiji Charter Oath, 1868 (as cited in[https://www.britannica.com/event/Charter-Oath](https://www.britannica.com/event/Charter-Oath)).

1. Based on the passage, the Meiji government’s primary strategy for industrialization was to

Explanation: The oath calls for seeking knowledge “throughout the world” and establishing deliberative assemblies, showing selective Western adoption.

2. The Charter Oath’s emphasis on “deliberative assemblies” and “public opinion” most directly reflects

Explanation: The call for deliberative assemblies mirrors Western parliamentary models Japan studied during the Meiji era.

3. The Meiji government’s approach to industrialization, as outlined in the passage, most directly resulted in

Explanation: Meiji reforms led to rapid industrialization, military modernization, and Japan’s emergence as an imperial power.

4. Which of the following best illustrates the broader impact of state-led industrialization programs like Japan’s Meiji Restoration during the period c. 1750–1900?

Explanation: Japan’s success demonstrated that deliberate state policy could accelerate industrialization outside the West.

Excerpt from Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776):

"It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest. We address ourselves, not to their humanity but to their self-love, and never talk to them of our own necessities but of their advantages."

Source: Adam Smith, An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations, 1776 (as cited in[https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3300/3300-h/3300-h.htm](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3300/3300-h/3300-h.htm)).

1. Based on the passage, Adam Smith’s economic philosophy most strongly supported which government policy toward industry?

Explanation: Smith argues that self-interest, not government direction, drives economic activity.

2. Smith’s emphasis on self-interest as the driver of economic activity most directly challenged which earlier economic system?

Explanation: Smith rejected mercantilist state intervention in favor of free markets guided by individual self-interest.

3. The ideas expressed in the passage most strongly influenced which approach to industrialization in 19th-century Britain?

Explanation: Britain largely followed laissez-faire principles in the early phases of industrialization.

4. Which of the following best illustrates the broader impact of laissez-faire economic thought, as represented in the passage, during the period c. 1750–1900?

Explanation: While influential early on, the social costs of industrialization eventually prompted greater state intervention (e.g., Factory Acts).

Excerpt from a British parliamentary report on joint-stock companies (1844):

"The principle of limited liability... has been found to be of great advantage in enabling persons to embark capital in undertakings of great public utility... without risking their whole fortunes... It has led to the formation of many companies for the construction of railways, docks, and other works of great national importance."

Source: Report of the Select Committee on Joint Stock Companies, 1844 (as cited in[https://parlipapers.proquest.com](https://parlipapers.proquest.com)).

1. Based on the passage, the introduction of limited liability most directly encouraged

Explanation: Limited liability allowed investors to risk only the amount they invested, encouraging bigger projects like railroads.

2. The growth of joint-stock companies with limited liability most strongly contributed to which economic development?

Explanation: Joint-stock companies with limited liability enabled the massive capital formation needed for railroads, factories, and infrastructure.

3. The passage’s reference to “undertakings of great public utility” suggests that limited liability companies were viewed as

Explanation: The report highlights benefits for public works like railways and docks.

4. Which of the following best illustrates the broader impact of financial innovations like limited liability during the period c. 1750–1900?

Explanation: Limited liability was crucial for funding the large-scale projects that defined the Industrial Age.

Excerpt from Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, The Communist Manifesto (1848):

"The bourgeoisie... has pitilessly torn asunder the motley feudal ties... It has resolved personal worth into exchange value... It has created enormous cities... It has agglomerated population... It has created more massive and more colossal productive forces than have all preceding generations together."

Source: Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, The Communist Manifesto, 1848 (as cited in[https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1848/communist-manifesto/](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1848/communist-manifesto/)).

1. Based on the passage, Marx and Engels viewed the bourgeoisie (capitalist class) as

Explanation: The passage acknowledges the bourgeoisie’s revolutionary role in ending feudalism while criticizing the new exploitation it created.

2. The reference to resolving “personal worth into exchange value” most directly criticizes which aspect of industrial capitalism?

Explanation: Marx and Engels argue that capitalism turns human qualities and labor into commodities measured only by exchange value.

3. Marx and Engels’ analysis in the passage most strongly influenced which later development?

Explanation: The Communist Manifesto became a foundational text for socialist and labor movements in the late 19th and 20th centuries.

4. Which of the following best illustrates the broader impact of critiques like those in the Communist Manifesto during the period c. 1750–1900?

Explanation: Marxist ideas inspired trade unions, socialist parties, and revolutionary movements across Europe and beyond.

Excerpt from a British government report on the effects of industrialization (1880s):

"While great progress has been made in the production of wealth and in the application of science to industry, the condition of the working classes in many districts remains unsatisfactory. Hours of labour are still excessive in some trades, housing is often inadequate, and the contrast between the wealth of the few and the poverty of the many has become more marked."

Source: Royal Commission on the Depression of Trade and Industry, Final Report, 1886 (as cited in[https://parlipapers.proquest.com](https://parlipapers.proquest.com)).

1. Based on the passage, which aspect of industrialization represented the greatest continuity from the early to late 19th century?

Explanation: The report notes that despite industrial progress, the gap between rich and poor had widened, showing continuity in inequality.

2. The passage’s mention of “great progress... in the production of wealth and in the application of science” highlights which major change brought by industrialization?

Explanation: The report acknowledges significant advances in wealth creation and scientific application to industry.

3. The report’s concern over “excessive” hours of labor and “inadequate” housing in the 1880s suggests that

Explanation: The fact that these issues were still being reported in the 1880s shows continuity of problems alongside change.

4. Which of the following best illustrates the broader pattern of continuity and change in the Industrial Age during the period c. 1750–1900?

Explanation: The passage shows both dramatic economic progress and the persistence of social problems, illustrating the complex mix of continuity and change.
