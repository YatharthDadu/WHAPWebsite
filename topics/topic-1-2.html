Topic 1.2

Developments in Dar al-Islam from c. 1200 to c. 1450

Learning objectives

  • Explain how systems of belief and their practices affected society from c. 1200 to c. 1450.
  • Explain the causes and effects of the rise of Islamic states over time.
  • Explain the effects of intellectual innovation in Dar al-Islam.
Progress 0% · Score 0

By 1200 the Abbasid Caliphate in Baghdad still held religious prestige, but real political power had fragmented. New Islamic political entities emerged, most dominated by Turkic peoples — showing continuity, innovation, and diversity.

Illustrative examples

  • Seljuk Empire — Turkic rulers in the Middle East and Anatolia.
  • Mamluk Sultanate (Egypt and Syria) — military-slave elite that later stopped the Mongols at Ain Jalut (1260).
  • Delhi Sultanate (northern India, from 1206) — Turkic and Afghan dynasties ruling a largely Hindu population.

The Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258 ended Abbasid political power. Islamic civilization, however, continued through successor states, law (Sharia), Arabic as a learned language, and trade.

Muslim rule expanded by military conquest, but the faith also spread through merchants, missionaries, and especially Sufis, who emphasized personal devotion and often adapted practice to local cultures in India, Central Asia, West Africa, and Southeast Asia.

Islam, Judaism, and Christianity continued to shape societies in Africa and Asia. In many Islamic states, non-Muslims (dhimmi) paid the jizya tax and lived under varying degrees of legal protection and restriction.

Muslim states encouraged intellectual innovation and transfers of knowledge. Earlier institutions such as the House of Wisdom in Baghdad left a legacy of translation and scholarship that continued in observatories, hospitals, and madrasas.

Illustrative examples

  • Mathematics — algebra, trigonometry, Hindu-Arabic numerals.
  • Nasir al-Din al-Tusi — astronomy and scientific method at Maragheh.
  • Medicine — hospitals, encyclopedias, preservation of Greek and Indian learning.
  • Literature and philosophy — Persian and Arabic poetry (including Sufi poets such as Rumi); engagement with Greek thought.

These transfers later helped carry knowledge into Europe via Spain, Sicily, and Crusader contact.
